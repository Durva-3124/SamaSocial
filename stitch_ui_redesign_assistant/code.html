Great question, Elena! This is one of the most frequently tested concepts on the Stanford CS229 midterm. Here is how to think about it both intuitively and in rigorous proof terms:

In the Transformer architecture, the Scaled Dot-Product Attention operates over queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$. Here is the canonical formulation:

// Matrix formulation of Self-Attention (Vaswani et al.)
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q · Kᵀ) / √d_k ) · V
stylus_note
Highlighted by you on slide 14:

"Why not divide by $d_k$ instead of $\sqrt{d_k}$? Remember: variance of the sum of independent products scales linearly with dimension, so the standard deviation scales with the square root!"

Why $\sqrt{d_k}$ is essential (The Variance Proof):

  • The Vanishing Gradient Problem: When dimension $d_k$ is large, the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax function into regions with extremely small gradients.
  • Statistical Normalization: Assuming components of $q$ and $k$ are independent random variables with mean $0$ and variance $1$, their dot product $\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$ has mean $0$ and variance $d_k$. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ normalizes the variance back to $1$.
💡

Professor's Pro-Tip for Homework 2 & Midterm

Pay special attention to the variance normalization proof on homework 2. If an exam question asks: "What happens if you omit the square root when $d_k = 512$?" — emphasize that attention degenerates into a one-hot 'hardmax', halting backpropagation.
